Coffee Culture

From Bean to Cup: A Visual Journey

by Justin Yu (36196657)  ·  May 2026

Begin

Walking through Melbourne on a weekday morning, you will see long queues outside many cafés. Coffee is a daily habit for many Australians, and Melbourne is often ranked among the world's best coffee cities.

However, the coffee in your cup has travelled a long way before reaching you. Most coffee beans in Australia are imported from countries near the equator, such as Brazil, Vietnam, Ethiopia and Colombia. Before they reach your local café, the beans cross several oceans and go through many roasting and shipping stages.

This project tells that story in three parts. We will look at where Australia's coffee comes from, where Australians drink it, and how its price has changed in recent years.

Part One

Where Does Our Coffee Come From?

Australia produces almost no coffee of its own. Less than 1% of beans roasted here are grown locally. The rest come from a small group of countries near the equator. To understand Australia's coffee, we first need to look at the world map.

The next four charts show this global picture: which countries produce the most coffee, where Australia gets its supply, and the difference between the two main species, Arabica and Robusta.

01 · Global Supply

A belt around the equator

Most of the world's coffee is grown within 25 degrees of the equator. Three countries — Brazil, Vietnam and Colombia — together produce more than half of the world's coffee.

Source: USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (PSD), 2024

02 · Top Producers

Three countries, half the world

Brazil alone produces about one-third of the world's coffee. This is a much higher share than any single country has in most other staple crops.

03 · Australia's coffee map

Where our beans come from and how it has changed

Brazil's share of Australia's coffee imports has grown steadily over the past ten years, while Vietnam's share has decreased. The supply has also become more concentrated: in 2015, "other" countries supplied almost half of Australia's coffee, but today this share is only about one third.

Vis 03 · AU import shares 2015–2025

Source: UN Comtrade Database (HS 0901, Australia as reporter, 2015–2025)

04 · Arabica vs Robusta

Two beans, two coffee worlds

Coffee comes from two main species: Arabica and Robusta. Arabica has a more complex flavour and is preferred for drinking. Robusta is easier to grow and has about twice the caffeine. This chart plots each country by total production and Arabica share. Colombia, Ethiopia and Honduras grow only Arabica. Vietnam and Indonesia mostly grow Robusta. Brazil is the only country that produces large amounts of both species.

Vis 04 · Country position by Arabica share

Source: USDA PSD Online, 2025 marketing year (estimates)

Part Two

Where We Drink It

After the beans arrive, they are distributed across Australia. Australians drink coffee at about twice the global per-capita average. However, coffee culture is not the same across the country. Some cities have very active café scenes, while others rely more on instant coffee.

The next four charts look at Australia in detail: by state, by city, and finally by price.

05 · States

Where the cafés are densest

New South Wales has the largest total number of cafés and restaurants. However, after adjusting for population, Victoria ranks first, followed closely by the ACT. The Northern Territory has the lowest density, less than half of Victoria's.

Vis 05 · Cafés per 10,000 residents

Sources: ABS CABEE (ANZSIC class 4511 "Cafés and Restaurants", June 2025); ABS Estimated Resident Population, June 2025

06 · Per Capita

Victoria leads the ranking

When measured per capita, the ranking changes. Victoria ranks first with 25.3 cafés and restaurants per 10,000 residents. The ACT and NSW follow closely. The Northern Territory has only about a third of Victoria's density. This suggests that Australia's coffee culture is mainly concentrated in coastal areas.

Vis 06 · Density ranked by state

Sources: ABS CABEE (ANZSIC class 4511 "Cafés and Restaurants", June 2025); ABS Estimated Resident Population, June 2025

07 · Cafés by City

Where most cafés are located

Melbourne and Sydney together have about 6,000 cafés, more than the other three capital cities combined. Melbourne ranks first in both total count and per-capita density. Most cafés in Australia are independent rather than chain-owned. Only 5.7% of Melbourne's cafés belong to a national chain, compared to 12.4% in Perth. Australia's coffee culture is mainly independent and concentrated in the southeast.

Vis 07 · 5-city café bubbles

Source: OpenStreetMap (amenity=cafe), queried via Overpass API for Greater Capital City bounding boxes, May 2026

08 · Imports and Prices

Imports rise, prices double

Between 2015 and 2025, Australia's coffee imports grew by 23%, from 100,000 to 123,000 tonnes. During the same period, the Arabica price more than doubled. This was caused by frost damage in Brazil, La Niña rains, and tight global supply. As a result, Australia's total import bill jumped from US$481 million to US$1.02 billion. Australians paid about twice as much for only a quarter more coffee.

Vis 08 · Imports vs Arabica price

Sources: UN Comtrade (HS 0901, Australia imports, 2015–2025); World Bank Pink Sheet (Coffee Arabica, monthly average)

Part Three

The Story Behind the Cup

Behind every $4.50 flat white there is a long chain of farmers, shippers, roasters and baristas. The price has risen faster than wages for most of the past decade. In this final part, we look at the journey of the beans, the long-term café sales trend, the price changes, and how cafés differ between cities.

09 · Flow Map   ·

From origin country to Australia

This map shows the origin countries of Australia's coffee imports in 2025. Each curved line connects an origin country to Australia. The thicker the line, the more coffee was imported from that country. Brazil alone supplied about 30,000 tonnes, nearly a quarter of the total. The line style shows the main species grown in that country: solid lines for Arabica-dominant countries (such as Colombia, Ethiopia, Honduras and Papua New Guinea), and dashed lines for Robusta-dominant countries (such as Vietnam, India, Uganda and Indonesia).

Vis 09 · Flow map (focus)

Sources: UN Comtrade (HS 0901, Australia imports, 2025); USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (Arabica vs Robusta species shares by country)

10 · Five years of cafés

From lockdown to landmark

This heatmap shows Australia's café, restaurant and takeaway turnover from June 2020 to June 2025. The lowest point was August 2021 (A$3.18 billion) during the Delta lockdowns. By May 2025, monthly turnover had risen to A$5.61 billion, a 76% increase from the trough. The ABS marked this series as ceased after June 2025, so this heatmap covers the entire final dataset.

Vis 10 · Café turnover heatmap

Source: ABS Retail Trade, Australia (Cat. 8501.0), Cafes, restaurants and takeaway food services, seasonally adjusted, Jun 2020 – Jun 2025 (final release)

11 · Price and Inflation

Inflation, but with a coffee spike

Australia's general CPI changed between 1.9% and 4.6% over the past year, and food prices remained around 3%. However, the coffee, tea and cocoa category increased much more. It peaked at +16.4% in October 2025, more than four times the headline rate. By March 2026 the rate had decreased to +10.7%, but this was still about double the All Groups figure.

Vis 11 · CPI annual change

Source: ABS Consumer Price Index, Australia, Mar 2026 (Table 3, Coffee/tea/cocoa expenditure class). Monthly CPI introduced Apr 2025.

12 · Five-City Comparison

Comparing five capital cities

This chart compares the five largest capital cities on two measures: the share of cafés that belong to national chains (horizontal axis) and the total number of cafés (vertical axis). The bubble size shows density per 10,000 residents. The quadrant lines are drawn at the data's midpoints (8.5% chain share and 1,500 total cafés). The chart shows three groups. Melbourne and Sydney form a "coffee capital" group: large markets with mostly independent cafés. Brisbane and Perth have smaller markets with more chains. Adelaide is small but still mostly independent. Melbourne has the highest density of all five cities.

Vis 12 · City coffee culture quadrant

Source: OpenStreetMap (amenity=cafe, brand tag for chain detection), Greater Capital City bounding boxes, May 2026

Australia's coffee story has many sides. From farms near the equator to cafés in Melbourne and Sydney, every cup is the end of a long supply chain. It involves global market forces, seasonal changes, and a daily habit shared by millions of people.
